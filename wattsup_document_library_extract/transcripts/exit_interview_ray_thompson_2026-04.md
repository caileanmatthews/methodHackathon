**INTERVIEWER:** Thanks for doing this, Ray. Thirty-eight years. Where do you want to start?

**RAY:** Wherever. I've got stories. Most of them are about Harlan Creek, honestly. That station's out on the ridge, wind comes right across it, and it's got the oldest stuff we own.

**INTERVIEWER:** Let's start there. What should the next person know about Harlan Creek?

**RAY:** Two things. B-12, the Allied oil breaker on the 69-4 line, and the tap changer on T1. Those two will eat a young tech alive if nobody tells them.

**INTERVIEWER:** Start with B-12.

**RAY:** Okay. So B-12 is an Allied BOC-69, early eighties. Allied's long gone, you can't call anybody. Good breaker, honestly, but it's got a personality. In the cold, it gets lazy on the trip. You send it a trip and it either takes forever or it just sits there and doesn't open, then you hit it again and it goes.

**INTERVIEWER:** Why does it do that?

**RAY:** Trip latch. The latch and the roller. The factory grease Allied put on there, it turns to varnish after enough years, and when it's real cold, single digits, it gets stiff like taffy. The trip coil hits the latch and the latch just doesn't want to let go. The manual's got a whole cold weather section that's supposed to cover this, and I'll bet you money nobody has those pages. I never saw them in thirty years.

**INTERVIEWER:** So what's the fix?

**RAY:** First thing, before you even touch the mechanism, check the cabinet heaters. Open the cabinet and put your hand in there. It should feel warmer than outside. If it's the same as outside, your heater's dead and that's half your problem right there. Every time B-12 has acted up in the cold, the heater was out or the heater breaker was tripped. 2009 it was the thermostat. Later it was the breaker in the cabinet. Heat is what keeps that grease soft enough to work.

**INTERVIEWER:** And the second thing?

**RAY:** Then the latch. But you do that right. Breaker out of service, isolated, grounded, control power off, and you dump both springs. Both. People forget the opening spring. Then you clean every bit of that old grease off the latch and the roller and the pins with solvent, and put Mobilgrease 28 on it. Thin. We stopped using the Allied grease, you can't get it anyway. Engineering signed off on Mobil 28 for these back in '14 after I fought them on it. You have to get all the old stuff off, don't mix it.

**INTERVIEWER:** Anything else on the latch?

**RAY:** Yeah, the adjusting screw. On this one, the latch engagement screw backs itself off over time. Vibration, I guess, wind, every operation. In 2016 I found it at forty thousandths. Spec is sixty to ninety. When it backs off, it messes with how the trip coil hits the latch. So check the engagement with a feeler gauge, set it back in the middle of spec, and tighten that locknut down hard. I'd check it every single time you're in there.

**INTERVIEWER:** How do you know you've actually fixed it?

**RAY:** Time it. Put the timer on it. Manual says three cycles max to contact part. When it's healthy it runs about two and a half, two point six. If it's over three, you're not done, don't put it back in service. And do a minimum trip voltage test, it's got to trip at 70 volts DC. If it'll trip at 70 it'll trip on a cold night.

**INTERVIEWER:** What's the mistake you see people make?

**RAY:** The big one is operators hitting the trip over and over. When that breaker doesn't open, the trip coil stays energized because the 52a contact never opens. That coil is only built for a quick shot. You hold it there, or you hit it four or five times, you burn it up. That's exactly what happened in 2016. Ops hit it four times, coil burned, and then you've got a breaker that can't trip at all on a line that might be faulting. So if B-12 doesn't open on the first try, stop. Call the tech out. Don't keep sending it.

**INTERVIEWER:** If someone's out there at night and a storm's coming, what would you tell them?

**RAY:** If the heater's dead and it's going below about ten degrees, I wouldn't trust B-12 to clear a fault on its own. I'd call system ops and ask them to take it out and carry the 69-4 load from the other end. They can do that from Bel Air. Then do the latch job. If you can't get an outage, at least get heat back in that cabinet. And write it down this time so the next guy knows.

**INTERVIEWER:** Let's talk about T1. The tap changer.

**RAY:** Right. T1's LTC. In 2024, after I'd mostly stopped doing Harlan, the old Allied control on it died. Kevin put a new microprocessor control in. Nice control. But here's the thing nobody caught. The old Allied control, the bandwidth dial on it reads plus or minus. So when the sheet says 0.6, that's 0.6 above and 0.6 below. That's a band of 1.2 volts total.

**INTERVIEWER:** And the new one?

**RAY:** The new ones, you put in the whole width. So if you type 0.6 in the new control, you just gave it a band of 0.6 total. Half of what it had. And each tap step on that changer is three quarters of a volt. So now one step is bigger than the whole band. It goes up a step, it's too high. Goes down a step, it's too low. Back and forth, all night. It's hunting. You'll see the counter just running.

**INTERVIEWER:** Did you tell anyone?

**RAY:** I mentioned it to somebody in the lunchroom, I don't know if it went anywhere. The fix is simple. It needs at least 1.2 in the new control to match what it had. I'd put it at 1.5 myself, gives you some room, and the book wants at least one and a half times the step anyway. Keep the set point at 122 and the delay at 45. And somebody should look at the LTC oil, because all that extra switching is wearing the contacts and making gas.
